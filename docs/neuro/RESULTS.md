Reading:

- **With no unrecorded common input, precision is essentially perfect** (1.0 up to 10 minutes, one
  false positive at 20). Conditioning each unit on its own past — the MCI step — is what stops
  autocorrelation from manufacturing edges between independent but strongly autocorrelated trains.
- **Common input costs precision, and recall is untouched.** Read down the table at fixed duration:
  recall is identical at every latent gain, while precision falls from 1.0 to about 0.4 as the
  hidden drive strengthens. Violating causal sufficiency does not hide real synapses; it adds fake
  ones. A weak latent (gain 1) is absorbed entirely — conditioning on a unit's own past is a good
  proxy for a slow shared drive — but that defence is overwhelmed by gain 2 and broken by gain 3.
- **The false positives are lagged, not contemporaneous.** Because the latent drive is itself
  autocorrelated, its footprint is not confined to the lag-0 slice where an FCI `<->` would flag it.
  This is the hard case: it looks exactly like a directed, time-resolved synaptic effect, which is
  what E2 tries (and, as it turns out, fails) to sort out.
- **Recall is a statistical-power question and it binds hard.** Recovered synapses are consistently
  the strong ones — the missed ones are three to four times weaker — and recall climbs with
  recording length before plateauing near 0.67. On real data the detectability threshold, not the
  algorithm, is what bounds the result.

## E2 — Do the certificates separate real synapses from confounded ones?

Each discovered edge gets a common-input tipping point: the fraction of variance an unrecorded
common input must explain **in both units** to erase the edge, benchmarked against the shared
variance actually observed among recorded pairs.

- observed shared-input benchmark: **0.0003**
- edges reported: 23 (9 genuine, 14 confounding-induced)
- median tipping point, genuine edges: **0.0149**
- median tipping point, false edges: **0.0134**
- separation (genuine − false): **0.00145**

**This one is a negative result, and it is worth stating plainly.** The tipping point is computed
correctly and means what it says, but it does *not* separate genuine synapses from
confounding-induced edges — the two medians are nearly identical. Nor is the benchmark doing useful
work: pairwise spike-count correlations at 5 ms bins are of order 1e-4, because most of a unit's
variance is point-process noise rather than shared drive, so essentially every edge clears it and
is labelled robust.

The diagnosis is a units mismatch. The tipping point is a requirement on the **log-intensity**
scale — how much of the drive a latent must supply — while the benchmark measures association on
the **spike-count** scale, which badly understates shared input at cortical rates and fine bins.
The two are not comparable, and the comparison is exactly what the "robust" verdict rests on.

What this does *not* undermine: the tipping point itself, which is exact for the linear-Gaussian
null and is a correct statement of what it would take to explain an edge away (verified against
simulated confounding in `tests/test_neuro_connectivity.py`). What it needs is a
point-process-calibrated benchmark — the shared drive implied by a latent of a given strength on
the intensity scale, not the correlation it induces in counts. Until that exists, the
`robust`/`fragile` split from `functional_connectivity` should not be relied on for spike data.
The machinery and the reporting are in place; the calibration is not.

## E3 — When does the mesoscopic model license micro-level causal claims?

200 units in two areas. The coupling scale moves the mesoscopic **loop gain** — the largest real
eigenvalue of the mean-field Jacobian. For each setting the spiking network is run under whole-area
silencing and driving, and compared against the population model's equilibrium `do()`.

| coupling scale | loop gain | stability margin | max error (Hz) | equilibria | verdict | worst |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 0.0394 | 0.961 | 1.03 | 1 | IDENTIFIED | drive(PMd) |
| 0.45 | 0.119 | 0.881 | 1.76 | 1 | IDENTIFIED | drive(PMd) |
| 0.6 | 0.212 | 0.788 | 2.86 | 1 | BOUNDED | drive(PMd) |
| 0.75 | 0.324 | 0.676 | 4.46 | 1 | BOUNDED | drive(PMd) |
| 0.9 | 0.469 | 0.531 | 6.46 | 1 | BOUNDED | drive(PMd) |
| 1 | 0.602 | 0.398 | 17.1 | 1 | EMPIRICAL | observational |
| 1.15 | 0.488 | 0.512 | 32.4 | 1 | EMPIRICAL | observational |

**This is the main result.** The commutation error grows monotonically with the loop gain, from
under 1 Hz to tens of Hz — and the stability margin stays *positive the whole way*. A model
selected by the usual criterion (is the mean-field fixed point stable?) would have passed every one
of these settings, including the ones where the population model is wrong by 30 Hz.

So mesoscopic stability is **not sufficient** for mesoscopic causal claims. The commutation error
has to be measured, which requires the micro model — the practical consequence being that a
population-level causal claim can only be trusted against a simulator calibrated to the spiking
data, or within an explicitly stated error bound. That is what the certificate reports.

## E4 — Which perturbations does a population model simply not have?

BOUNDED, because of:

> 2 of 7 interventions have no mesoscopic counterpart (omega undefined): a population model cannot answer a perturbation targeting part of an area

- liftable: observational, silence(M1), drive(M1), silence(PMd), drive(PMd)
- **not liftable**: silence(half of M1), silence(half of PMd)

Silencing *part* of an area has no mesoscopic counterpart: the macro state does not resolve which
units were clamped, so the abstraction map `omega` is undefined there. This is the precise sense in
which a population model cannot answer a targeted-perturbation question — exactly the question an
optogenetic or intracortical-microstimulation experiment asks. The certificate reports it rather
than silently substituting an area-wide intervention.

## Real data

This program is designed to run on the massively parallel reach-to-grasp recordings
(Brochier et al., *Sci. Data* 4:170055, 2018; DOI 10.12751/g-node.f83565) via
`causalrl.neuro.io.load_dataset`, which reads a local Neo/NIX copy. **No experimental data was used
here**: the environment this ran in has no network route to `gin.g-node.org` (or DANDI, CRCNS,
Zenodo, or OSF), so every number above is simulator-based. The loader deliberately never downloads;
it names the DOI and source instead.

The simulator is not a substitute for that data — it is the answer key the methods had to pass
before being pointed at recordings where no answer key exists.
